Dark Matter Labs / Regen Food Systems

Proving the resilience
case for regenerative soil.

A rapid field study, ready to go, ahead of next summer.

Why this, why now

This year's drought gave the UK a live test of the soil-health thesis — industrial farms hit hard, regenerative soil holding its moisture. Right now that's a strong anecdote. It needs to become evidence.

Some conventional farmers are already switching of their own accord — not for ideology, but because they watched a neighbour's soil outperform theirs while input costs kept climbing.

Left unanswered, that gap could just as easily be filled by capital chasing it on its own terms. Answered well, it gives government and industry a floor to act on, not just a story to repeat.

Method

Rapid field research, not a desk study

Fast access through existing relationships

Dark Matter Labs' own organic wholesaler relationships (Wholegood, Langridge Organic) and Compass's Foodbuy supply network give two independent, fast routes to real farmers — not a cold-call exercise. This is the single biggest lever for speed and cost.

Stratified, not exhaustive

Around 15–18 farm visits across a handful of UK regions and farm types, not an attempt at a national census. Enough to be credible, not so much it misses the April/May deadline.

Triangulated against public data

Interview and site-visit findings checked against Defra's June Agricultural Survey, Soil Association reporting, AHDB yield data and Environment Agency drought records — so the published findings aren't resting on interviews alone.

Answers the obvious counter-argument directly

Sir Charles Godfrey's (Oxford) caution is the right one to design against from day one: pushing regenerative transition too fast risks plugging the resulting yield gap with worse sourcing elsewhere. The methodology measures net system impact, not just the upside case at the farm gate — and says so plainly in the published output.

Delivery

Two people, on the ground — work done and published by May

The large majority of the time is spent on the ground — farm visits, structured interviews, site data collection — across a handful of UK regions. Not a desk-based pair: a field researcher doing the bulk of the travel and interviewing, paired with a lead who structures the research design, handles wholesaler and farmer access, and leads the analysis and write-up. ~58 working days each, concentrated in a focused Dec–Feb fieldwork window.

Professor Paul Behrens (Oxford) is likely to join part of the fieldwork directly — a collaboration already being planned separately — bringing independent academic credibility to the published findings.

Partnering with Compass.

This sits squarely inside Compass's own regenerative-sourcing target and Our Planet Promise commitments — and directly addresses a gap Compass has itself identified: the ambition is there, but supply-chain volume isn't catching up fast enough. It's equally core to Dark Matter Labs' own food-missions work and the wider push toward a more sovereign, shock-resistant UK food system.

  1. Farmer and wholesaler access via Foodbuy, to widen and speed up the sample alongside Dark Matter Labs' existing organic wholesaler relationships.
  2. Levy as a possible live case study — a track record trialling new approaches that could turn a finding into an actual pilot, not just a published one.
  3. Credibility and challenge, reviewing the work specifically against the yield-gap risk before it's published.
  4. Funding: a direct contribution of £45,000 toward the £84,616 total — just over half — with Dark Matter Labs covering the rest from its own resourcing.
