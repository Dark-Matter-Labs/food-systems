A Neighbourhood Food Model, drawn from Walworth, Southwark

Four jobs a neighbourhood food system has to do.

A borough sets policy and a hub moves volume, but the neighbourhood is where people actually spend, cook and eat. Pembroke House built an explicit model for doing this well in Walworth. This page pulls out what transfers to our own neighbourhood-scale work.

Below the borough, above the household

Why the neighbourhood is where it gets real

Walworth is a single neighbourhood in Southwark, population around 45,000. Pembroke House, a local charity, didn't wait for a borough-wide strategy to name what a good food system there would actually require. It named four jobs the system has to do, and started building test sites for each one.

Everything below is Pembroke House's own framing of the Walworth Neighbourhood Food Model, reported here with the sources it's drawn from. Where it connects to our own neighbourhood work, in Kilburn and elsewhere, that's marked separately.

Job one

Celebrate a neighbourhood's diverse food cultures

What's already being cooked, sold and shared is the starting material, not something to standardise away before the "real" work begins.

Pembroke House's Walworth Food Map set out to spotlight the people and initiatives behind the neighbourhood's own diverse food system: walking the length of Walworth Road logging food tied to different cultures, family traditions and the area's own history, from independent restaurants like the Colombian eatery Amigos to long-running stalls and shops. The long-term ambition for Pembroke House's planned site on Walworth Road is explicitly rooted in community engagement, economic empowerment and cultural preservation together, not as three separate goals.

What transfers: map a neighbourhood's food culture before writing a strategy for it. The map is the asset, not a preamble to one.
Job two

Keep wealth in the neighbourhood

The money residents already spend on food every year dwarfs the grant funding chasing food insecurity. The opportunity is in where the existing spend goes, not only in raising new funding.

Pembroke House estimates Walworth's own residents spend around £94 million a year on food. Their own analysis found that converting just 1% of that spending into local expenditure would exceed the entire combined budget of every food-insecurity project currently funded in the neighbourhood.

Job three

Community-led enterprise, and better-paid food jobs

Pay is treated as part of the intervention, not an afterthought to it, and the enterprise sits somewhere people actually walk past.

Locally owned businesses and co-ops

Strong local supply chains, new food projects, locally owned food businesses, cooperatives, collaborations and partnerships, named directly as the mechanism for keeping wealth local, not a side effect of it.

A living-wage anchor

The Walworth Living Room, Pembroke House's own café, runs on London Living Wage terms. The pay itself is part of the food intervention, not just the food served.

A high-street site

A planned community kitchen of around 2,626 sq ft at 182–202 Walworth Road, developed with Fabrix and given unanimous planning consent as part of the wider scheme: food infrastructure claiming prominent high-street space, not tucked round the back.

Job four

Bring people together around food

A loose alliance that others can join, built on what's already there, rather than one organisation holding the work.

Pembroke House describes the model as a neighbourhood food alliance of people who are growing, supplying, cooking and sharing food, not a single body running it on everyone else's behalf. An Action Learning Group brings around ten local food initiatives and enterprises together to learn from each other's practical problems, rather than compete quietly for the same small pot. The guiding instinct throughout is to identify underused skills and assets already in the community, before adding new provision on top of them.

Where this connects: shared authorship, not one side holding power

An alliance that others can join rather than a single commissioned body is close to the governance logic already running through our own Camden work: shared authorship rather than one side holding power over the other.

Whole system, not single project

How the model actually gets built

The Southwark Food Action Alliance, working through Sustain's Food Power programme, frames the model as a whole-system approach: production, distribution, preparation and consumption treated as inseparable from employment, housing and health outcomes, not run through one food project in isolation.

It's built through naming actual test sites to trial and learn, then feeding what works back up through local, borough and regional tiers rather than scaling a single pilot by assertion. Southwark's own Land Commission has since said the model should be resourced and replicated across the borough's other neighbourhoods, and Pembroke House has fed it directly into Southwark's Sustainable Food Strategy and Action Plan.

Where this connects: the same ladder, one rung down

Local test site, borough strategy, regional replication is the same ladder our own Hubs & Markets Working Group runs between boroughs and London-wide, just starting one rung lower, at the neighbourhood itself.

Already underway, one street at a time

What this means for our own neighbourhood work

Camden's own Kilburn High Road proposal is already DML's live attempt at this exact scale: working with the businesses and organisations already on the high road, rather than starting from a blank page.

The four jobs above are a useful test for it, and for any neighbourhood work that follows: has anyone actually mapped what's there, is the wealth argument made in pounds rather than principle, is there an enterprise the model puts real money through, and is there a way in for people who aren't already in the room.

A working check, not a template

Before calling it a neighbourhood food model

  • Has anyone mapped the food culture already there, or are we assuming it?
  • Do we know how much residents already spend on food each year, and how much of it currently leaves the neighbourhood?
  • Is there at least one local enterprise or co-op this model would put real money through, not just serve?
  • Is there a recurring, low-friction way people actually meet over food, not just a steering group minute?
  • Is it one organisation claiming the work, or an alliance that others can join?
