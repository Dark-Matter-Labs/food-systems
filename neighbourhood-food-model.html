Working note, October 2026
A Neighbourhood Food Model, Camden Food Mission

Four jobs for a Camden neighbourhood, starting with Kilburn.

Camden's food mission has a new focus: build resilience at the neighbourhood level, the way the NHS's own neighbourhood approach asks each local authority to. Camden has five neighbourhoods, and Kilburn High Road is where this starts. Here's what each one actually has to do, tested against a neighbourhood that's already a few years ahead: Walworth, Southwark.

Neighbourhood, borough, system

Camden's own shape for this

Not DML's framework. Camden's own, set out directly, still being worked through rather than signed off, which is exactly why it belongs here in the open rather than locked in a single document.

Neighbourhood (Camden has five)

Local partners, local physical and relational infrastructure, growing space, kitchens, local knowledge brought together, strengths and gaps identified, a food offer designed for that neighbourhood specifically.

Borough

The food hub, enabling the neighbourhood infrastructure. Partner organisations, each holding expertise in one area, healthy eating, sustainability, affordability, setting minimum standards and backing locally led innovation.

System

The Food Partnership Steering Group as the glue holding the levels together.

The North Star, five years out: a food agenda that holds its own outside the council, protected from changing political priorities rather than reliant on any one administration.
The first of five

Why Kilburn goes first

A model for the neighbourhood level means nothing until it's tried in one. Kilburn High Road is DML's own live attempt: working with the businesses and community organisations already on the high road, rather than starting from a blank page.

Whatever Kilburn's own food offer turns out to be, it doesn't need to be invented from scratch. A neighbourhood a few years further along, Walworth in Southwark, has already tested most of the ground: four jobs, lifted from Pembroke House's own Neighbourhood Food Model, worth testing against Kilburn and then each of Camden's other four neighbourhoods in turn.

Open Kilburn High Road
Job one

Celebrate a neighbourhood's diverse food cultures

What's already being cooked, sold and shared is the starting material, not something to standardise away before the "real" work begins.

Pembroke House's Walworth Food Map set out to spotlight the people and initiatives behind the neighbourhood's own diverse food system: walking the length of Walworth Road logging food tied to different cultures, family traditions and the area's own history, from independent restaurants like the Colombian eatery Amigos to long-running stalls and shops. The long-term ambition for Pembroke House's planned site on Walworth Road is explicitly rooted in community engagement, economic empowerment and cultural preservation together, not as three separate goals.

For Kilburn: has anyone mapped Kilburn High Road's own food culture, street by street, the way Walworth did? The map is the asset, not a preamble to one.
Job two

Keep wealth in the neighbourhood

The money residents already spend on food every year dwarfs the grant funding chasing food insecurity. The opportunity is in where the existing spend goes, not only in raising new funding.

Pembroke House estimates Walworth's own residents spend around £94 million a year on food. Their own analysis found that converting just 1% of that spending into local expenditure would exceed the entire combined budget of every food-insecurity project currently funded in the neighbourhood.

For Kilburn: Kilburn's own version of this number doesn't exist yet. It's the first useful thing to go and find, before the borough-level food hub can aggregate anyone's demand. See the Hubs & Markets Working Group.
Job three

Community-led enterprise, and better-paid food jobs

Pay is treated as part of the intervention, not an afterthought to it, and the enterprise sits somewhere people actually walk past.

Locally owned businesses and co-ops

Strong local supply chains, new food projects, locally owned food businesses, cooperatives, collaborations and partnerships, named directly as the mechanism for keeping wealth local, not a side effect of it.

A living-wage anchor

The Walworth Living Room, Pembroke House's own café, runs on London Living Wage terms. The pay itself is part of the food intervention, not just the food served.

A high-street site

Walworth's planned community kitchen, around 2,626 sq ft at 182–202 Walworth Road, developed with Fabrix and given unanimous planning consent: food infrastructure claiming prominent high-street space, not tucked round the back. Kilburn High Road has the same kind of frontage going spare.

Job four

Bring people together around food

A loose alliance that others can join, built on what's already there, rather than one organisation holding the work.

Pembroke House describes the model as a neighbourhood food alliance of people who are growing, supplying, cooking and sharing food, not a single body running it on everyone else's behalf. An Action Learning Group brings around ten local food initiatives and enterprises together to learn from each other's practical problems, rather than compete quietly for the same small pot. The guiding instinct throughout is to identify underused skills and assets already in the community, before adding new provision on top of them.

Where this connects: shared authorship, not one side holding power

An alliance that others can join rather than a single commissioned body is close to the governance logic already running through our own Camden work: shared authorship rather than one side holding power over the other.

See Governing for Transformation.

Whole system, not single project

How Kilburn connects to the borough and the system

A neighbourhood food offer doesn't stand alone. It leans on a borough-wide hub and on partner organisations with real expertise, and it's held together by a partnership that can actually speak for the whole mission.

The food hub, the subject of our own Hubs & Markets Working Group, is what lets Kilburn's own demand aggregate with everyone else's rather than sit alone. Partner organisations hold the expertise, healthy eating, sustainability, affordability, that a single neighbourhood group shouldn't have to build from zero. And the Food Partnership Steering Group is meant to be the glue: not one side holding power over the other, the same shared-authorship logic already running through our own governance work with Camden.

A working check, not a template

Before calling it done in Kilburn

  • Has anyone mapped Kilburn's own food culture, street by street, or are we assuming it?
  • Do we know how much Kilburn residents already spend on food each year, and how much of it currently leaves the neighbourhood?
  • Is there at least one Kilburn enterprise or co-op this model would put real money through, not just serve?
  • Is there a recurring, low-friction way Kilburn residents actually meet over food, not just a steering group minute?
  • Does this plug into the borough hub and the Food Partnership, or sit alongside them?
