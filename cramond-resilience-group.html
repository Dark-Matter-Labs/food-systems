Working note, October 2026
Cramond and Barnton, Almond ward, City of Edinburgh

A resilience group for Cramond and Barnton.

When a storm, flood, long power cut or food shortage hits, the first help usually comes from neighbours. This note sets out how Ready Scotland says to organise that help, what we already have locally, and what the community council, the Scouts, Lauriston Farm and others could do together.

Why bother

What a resilience group is for

A community resilience group is a small, named set of local people who have agreed in advance how the area will look after itself for the first hours and days of an emergency, before or alongside the council and emergency services. It writes this down as a community emergency plan, keeps a list of local assets and volunteers, and practises.

It does not replace 999, the council or the emergency services. Ready Scotland describes it as complementary: the group knows the place and the people, the responders bring the powers and equipment. A plan also gives the council's resilience team a single local contact, which makes Cramond and Barnton easier to help.

We are well placed. Cramond and Barnton already have a community council, an active Scout group with halls and kit, churches and a school, and Lauriston Farm, one of Edinburgh's largest community food-growing sites, on our doorstep. What's missing is the plan that ties them together.

What the Scottish Government guide asks for

The process, step by step

The guide is organised as three steps, with annexes on insurance and on health and safety, and templates for a community emergency plan and household plans. We've added a step zero for forming the group.

  1. 00

    Form the group and agree its home

    Decide who convenes, who belongs and which body the group sits under. The simplest route is a working group of the community council, so it inherits the council's constitution, meetings and insurance conversation.

    • Agree a boundary: the community council area is the natural one.
    • Name a coordinator and a deputy, so there is always a second person.
    • Invite the Scouts, Lauriston Farm, churches, the school, residents' associations and businesses.
    • Tell the City of Edinburgh Council's resilience team and our Almond ward councillors the group exists.
  2. 01

    Make the plan

    Use the Ready Scotland community emergency plan template. It's a Word document you adapt with the area's own name and details.

    • Assess local risks. Start from the Community Risk Register published by the regional resilience partnership, then add what residents know: where water lies, which roads close, who loses power first.
    • List local assets. People, buildings, equipment, transport, skills, defibrillators, generators, food and growing land.
    • Reduce the impact before anything happens. Encourage every household to make its own emergency plan and kit, and people who rely on power or water to join the priority services registers.
    • Decide how we would respond and recover. Triggers for activating the plan, who calls whom, where people gather, how we communicate if mobiles and broadband are down.
  3. 02

    Respond and recover

    Agree what the group does in the first 72 hours and in the weeks after, and where its job stops.

    • Always call 999 first when life is at risk. The group supports, it does not lead the emergency response.
    • Activation: a named person can open a rest centre, start a phone tree or check on neighbours.
    • Coordinate with the council once it is involved, and keep a simple log of what was done and by whom.
    • Recovery: clean-up, food and welfare, and looking after volunteers afterwards.
  4. 03

    Practise and update

    A plan nobody has tested will fail on the night. The guide asks groups to practise and to keep contacts current.

    • Run a short tabletop exercise once a year, for example a winter storm with a three-day power cut.
    • Check the contact list every six months and after any change of key holder.
    • Review the plan after every real event and share what changed with the council.
    • Volunteers can take Ready Scotland Learn courses at no cost.

Food as infrastructure

Treating local food as part of the plan

Most community plans stop at blankets and phone trees. Cramond and Barnton could go further, because we have growing land and growers next door.

UK supermarkets typically hold a few days of stock and rely on just-in-time deliveries. A storm, fuel shortage, cyber attack on payments or a wider supply shock can empty local shelves faster than help arrives. A neighbourhood that knows where its food is grown, stored and cooked can feed its most exposed people for those first days, and recover faster afterwards.

This is the same idea Dark Matter Labs is working on in London: food treated like roads or water, with neighbourhoods each holding some of the capacity. See the neighbourhood food model for how that is being tried in Camden.

In a crisis

  • Lauriston Farm produce routed to a community kitchen in a church or Scout hall
  • A shared list of who has freezers, stores and cooking capacity
  • Meals delivered to people who can't get out, using the volunteer network
  • Agreed links with the nearest food banks so no one falls through the gap

Before any crisis

  • A growing site at the pony paddock or off Brae Park Road, run with the farm
  • Seed and tool library, and preserving skills days
  • Beavers and Cubs growing with the farm through badge work
  • A local food map: growers, shops, cafés, stores, kitchens

Before anyone volunteers

Things to know

The parts of the guide that are easy to skip and costly to get wrong.

Insurance

Ready Scotland calls community resilience work low risk, but asks groups to check third-party liability, motor and professional indemnity cover. Some councils have agreed that actions under a community emergency plan fall under the council's own liability cover, so ask the City of Edinburgh Council first. Community councils insured with Zurich Municipal should ask the insurer directly. Scouts' insurance covers Scouting activities, so check before it's relied on for anything else.

Health and safety

The guide's second annex covers volunteer safety. No one should put themselves at risk: no wading into floodwater, no chainsaw work without training, no climbing on roofs. Brief volunteers before they go out and know where everyone is.

Data protection

Hold the least personal data possible, with people's consent, stored securely and reviewed regularly. Avoid keeping our own list of "vulnerable people". Instead encourage people to join the priority services registers run by the energy network and Scottish Water, and ask the council how it shares information in an emergency.

Safeguarding

Young people can help prepare, grow and learn, but should not be frontline responders. Scouting activity stays under Scouts' safeguarding rules and adult-to-child ratios. Adults visiting vulnerable people may need PVG checks, so ask the council before setting up home visits.

Volunteer wellbeing

Responding to an emergency can be hard on volunteers. Lifelines Scotland offers resources to help groups look after their people during and after an event.

Funding

Ready Scotland notes community groups may be able to get funding. Places to ask: the council's community grants, the National Lottery's Awards for All Scotland, and local businesses for kit. Start with what the group needs, not what a funder offers.
