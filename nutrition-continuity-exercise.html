Discussion document, systemic risk and financial stability

Situation rooms on how a food shock lands on a balance sheet

Senior people in finance and policy already accept that climate and nature risk is systemic. That knowledge has not yet changed where capital goes. We propose a short series of small, director-level situation rooms, starting in early 2027. Each takes one near-term food-system disruption and traces it to the exposures the people in the room are responsible for.

Second draft by Milan and Adam, 15 October 2026, updated after our latest conversation.

What we are asking the partners for. A thumbs up on the structure, framing and first-room design below. Names to add to the shortlist, especially people who hold a balance sheet rather than a sustainability brief. And a nominee for the operational lead, if you have one.

Why the financial system is the entry point

Food is where climate, water, energy and trade risk turns concrete fastest. A thin harvest or a blocked chokepoint shows up within months as food inflation that interest rates can’t fix, as claims and withdrawn cover for insurers, as stressed agricultural loan books, as repricing in portfolios exposed to food and commodities, and as calls on public money. These are not 2050 scenarios. The Systems Transformation Hub’s risk outlook for September 2026 to March 2027 describes disruptions that could arrive inside one reporting cycle.

Finance is the right way in because the losses land there, and because the people who hold those balance sheets can move capital in ways no food-system actor can on their own.

The first room: where it starts and where it should end

Starting point. Participants already know the risk is systemic. What they lack is a concrete line from a near-term shock to their own book, and the words to make that case to their boards, supervisors and investors.

Destination. Each participant leaves with:

  • one decision within their own remit that they will test in the next six months, such as a stress-test assumption, an underwriting rule, a lending criterion or a stewardship ask
  • a short, plain account they can use with colleagues, regulators and investors of why not shifting capital lands losses on their own balance sheet
  • named follow-ups with others in the room.

No one outside the room holds the action

Rooms like this often fail when participants can point to an absent actor as the one with real power: “that’s for the ECB”, “the regulator would have to move first”. We design against that in two ways. We put the people with the remit in the room, and when the conversation drifts to someone absent, the facilitator brings it back to what the people present can do themselves.

A candidate scenario

Still to be chosen with the partners. As a starting point, one drawn from the near-term outlook and three cascades already in our model:

  1. A winter energy squeeze, with a chokepoint such as the Strait of Hormuz repricing LNG, fertiliser and freight insurance.
  2. Farmers cut nitrogen use ahead of spring planting, while a strong El Niño stresses several breadbaskets in the same season.
  3. Import-dependent food prices rise months later and food inflation stays high after energy prices ease.

How the room is designed

  • Small and senior. Eight to ten people at director level with executive power. How senior is a design choice: the top, one level below or two, depending on who holds the remit to act.
  • A host that lends weight. Ideally the European Central Bank or the European Investment Bank, at their offices. Neither has been asked yet.
  • A conversation with everyone first. A one-to-one interview with every participant beforehand, to understand their exposure, what they could change and how they are likely to react. The design of the day builds on what we hear.
  • Rehearsed. The team runs the session in full beforehand, playing the participants.
  • In finance language. Plain terms, near-term horizons, no debates about terminology.
  • Fixed slots for senior voices. Steering-board members and other senior contributors speak in set places on the agenda, each with a specific ask.
  • Repeatable. Later rooms reuse the format with a different shock or a different part of the financial system.

Who should be in the room

Our first shortlist leans on sustainability and climate leads. They are our natural allies and often the way in, but they rarely hold the balance sheet. For each institution we should aim for the person whose decisions would change under this scenario: a chief risk officer, a chief underwriting officer, a head of agricultural lending, a chief investment officer or a financial-stability director. The sustainability lead can open the door, and the seat should go to whoever holds the remit.

Keeping it moving

The steering board gives direction at three set points: the scenario and shortlist, the final design before the first room, and the debrief. Each review has a five-working-day window. Between those points, the operational lead decides within the agreed scope and budget, so the work never waits on one person’s diary.

Resourcing stays simple. Each partner receives a baseline, plus resource for the time its people actually spend.

Funding

We will raise money for the programme from public banks, private banks and philanthropies. Some institutions may prefer to contribute by hosting a room. The longer-term aim is for financial institutions to pay for rooms that serve them. The first ask goes to the Collaborative’s Berlin meeting in early December.

Questions for choosing

  • Are we preparing for a specific shock, testing a policy decision or building a recurring capability?
  • Which decisions should participants make during the session, and how much modelling do they need to make them?
  • What can an existing partner adapt, and what should be reusable afterwards?
  • Who owns preparation, facilitation and follow-through?
