Discussion document, nutrition continuity and financial stability

A first exercise on how a food shock becomes a financial one

We propose to run a facilitated exercise, in the first quarter of 2027, that puts financial institutions and food-system actors in one room with a single disruption scenario. They trace how it spreads into prices, insurance losses and public finances, find where today’s responses fall short, and leave with actions they own.

Drafted by Milan and Adam, 7 October 2026. For a funding decision at the Collaborative’s Berlin meeting in early December.

What we are asking for. Agreement on the scenario and format below, introductions into the financial sector, and a funding proposal for Berlin: either a six-month pilot or seed funding for a small delivery capability. Either should pay for a first exercise and a second, improved one.

Topic and use case

When food, water, energy and logistics come under pressure together, the question is whether people can keep eating well. If they can’t, the pressure doesn’t stay in the food system. It moves into sticky inflation that a central bank can’t treat, into claims and withdrawn cover for insurers, into weaker household and corporate balance sheets, and finally into public finances. Our Nutrition Cascade maps these pathways. It is qualitative, with illustrative weights and timings, and is a starting point for the exercise, not a forecast.

A candidate shock sequence

The sequence is still to be chosen. As a starting point, we suggest one drawn from the near-term outlook for September 2026 to March 2027 and three cascades already in the model:

  1. A winter energy squeeze, with a chokepoint such as the Strait of Hormuz repricing LNG, fertiliser and freight insurance.
  2. Farmers cut nitrogen use ahead of spring planting, while a strong El Niño stresses several breadbaskets in the same season.
  3. Import-dependent food prices rise months later, households trade protein for cheap calories, and food inflation stays high after energy prices ease.

Decisions participants would make

  • A central bank deciding whether to look through food inflation or tighten, knowing tightening falls hardest on the households already cutting protein.
  • Insurers and reinsurers deciding whether to reprice, exclude or keep covering crop, cargo and supply-chain risk.
  • Governments choosing between price support, reserve releases, export controls and protecting public food provision such as school meals.
  • Large food buyers, including public procurers, deciding whether to hold prices through forward contracts.

What the exercise should produce

A shared map of the transmission pathways the group recognises, a list of gaps where no one currently acts, and a short set of interventions with a named institution willing to take each forward. The scenario, roles and debrief method should be reusable, so the second exercise costs less than the first.

Audience

The financial sector is the way in. The capital team will test interest and ask what would make participation worth a senior person’s day. Candidates to test, none of them approached yet:

  • Insurers and reinsurers with agricultural, cargo or food-supply exposure, and Lloyd’s market participants.
  • Central banks and financial-stability bodies, including the networks that already work on climate risk, such as the NGFS.
  • Development and public banks financing food and agricultural infrastructure.

Around them sit the actors needed to make the scenario real: national food and agriculture ministries, a city or borough food partnership (the London work gives us Camden, Islington and Hackney), large retailers and public procurers, and logistics operators. Fifteen to forty people in total. Each should come with a decision they actually hold, not just a view.

Format and delivery

We suggest a structured tabletop: a facilitated half-day to two-day session that moves through the scenario at fixed decision points. It needs six to twelve weeks of preparation and is the cheapest serious format that still leaves participants with commitments. A short executive game could open it for senior financial participants. The other formats we reviewed are in the annex.

We would not build this ourselves. We will scope it with an experienced partner and check what existing tools can be adapted: the Food Alert simulation partners, who are waiting on this document; ASRA’s STEER systemic-risk tool; and the Social Simulations portfolio, including the Mobility Transition exercise we worked on with them.

Keeping it moving without a bottleneck

A credible exercise needs senior input, but it shouldn’t wait on any one person’s diary. So we propose that decision rights sit with the people doing the work.

The three review points are the scenario draft, the participant list and the debrief. Each has a five-working-day window. If no comment comes, the work goes ahead.

Introductions to financial institutions run through several people at once: the capital team, the delivery partner and the advisers. Drafts, the scenario and results live in one shared place, so anyone on the team can pick up the work. The plan should still run if any one of us is unavailable for a month.

Funding for Berlin

The longer-term aim is for financial institutions to pay for this work. Until then, we see two ways to start. Both are our working assumptions and will change once a partner quotes.

A six-month pilot

€115–200k

  • Partner design and facilitation of the first exercise, €40–80k
  • A second exercise reusing the format, €20–40k
  • Operational lead for six months, €45–60k
  • Venue, travel and write-up, €10–20k

Clear scope and end date. Stops after six months unless someone else pays.

Seed for a delivery capability

€150–250k over 12 months

  • Operational lead and a part-time analyst
  • A retainer with the delivery partner
  • The first exercise funded in full, the second co-funded by an institution

Keeps a team in place long enough to win paying clients. Asks more of the Collaborative up front.

Adviser time is assumed to be in kind. Participant time, often the largest real cost, is excluded from both.
