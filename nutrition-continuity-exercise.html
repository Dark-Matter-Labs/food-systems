Discussion document, systemic risk and financial stability

Situation rooms on how a food shock lands on a balance sheet

Senior people in finance and policy already accept that climate and nature risk is systemic. That knowledge has not yet changed where capital goes. We propose a short series of small, director-level situation rooms, starting in early 2027. Each takes one near-term food-system disruption and traces it to the exposures the people in the room are responsible for.

Third draft by Milan and Adam, 20 October 2026.

What we need from the team now. Please review the points below and let us know your availability to discuss them.

Why the financial system is the entry point

Food is where climate, water, energy and trade risk turns concrete fastest. A thin harvest or a blocked chokepoint shows up within months as food inflation that interest rates can’t fix, as claims and withdrawn cover for insurers, as stressed agricultural loan books, as repricing in portfolios exposed to food and commodities, and as calls on public money. These are not 2050 scenarios. The Systems Transformation Hub’s risk outlook for September 2026 to March 2027 describes disruptions that could arrive inside one reporting cycle.

Finance is the right way in because the losses land there, and because the people who hold those balance sheets can move capital in ways no food-system actor can on their own.

The case to them is a fiduciary one, not a moral one. Money is a claim on the future. When food, energy, water and geopolitical risks start feeding back on one another, the value of those claims becomes less certain, and most models still treat each risk on its own. The rooms ask participants to look at that coupling as a risk to their own book, and to price the value of continuity: keeping a city fed protects the value of everything else in it.

“I’m not inviting you to be kind. I’m just inviting you to be smart right now.”

Indy Johar, speaking to capital allocators on The Great Simplification ↗

Where the shock lands differs for each kind of institution, and so does what each can change. The table in Who should be in the room sets this out, group by group, with the institutions and seats we are aiming for.

Our Nutrition Cascade maps these pathways. It is qualitative, with illustrative weights and timings, and is a starting point for the scenario rather than a forecast.

The first room: where it starts and where it should end

Starting point. Participants already know the risk is systemic. What they lack is a concrete line from a near-term shock to their own book, and the words to make that case to their boards, supervisors and investors.

Destination. Each participant leaves with:

  • one decision within their own remit that they will test in the next six months, such as a stress-test assumption, an underwriting rule, a lending criterion or a stewardship ask
  • a short, plain account they can use with colleagues, regulators and investors of why not shifting capital lands losses on their own balance sheet
  • named follow-ups with others in the room.

The question that gets them there is the one an insurer would ask about a house fire. If this risk is plausible, how much of your book should go to managing it, and through what? Even a fraction of a per cent, allocated deliberately, is a decision. Participants don’t have to share our view of the risk. We ask them to bring their own model and test it against the scenario.

No one outside the room holds the action

Rooms like this often fail when participants can point to an absent actor as the one with real power: “that’s for the ECB”, “the regulator would have to move first”. We design against that in two ways. We put the people with the remit in the room, and when the conversation drifts to someone absent, the facilitator brings it back to what the people present can do themselves.

Options ready before the shock

A real food shock will open a short window when institutions are willing to act. The rooms should leave a set of prepared options for that window: fire-breaks that stop a cascade spreading, such as a civic reserve, a price-continuity hedge or forward demand contracts, each with an owner and a rough cost. Then, when the shock arrives, the work starts from options already prepared.

The scenario

We follow the sequence Indy sets out in the Nutrition Continuity paper. The room starts in spring, with the shock already under way after a winter of high energy and fertiliser prices.

  1. Spring. Farmers have cut nitrogen use ahead of planting, and a strong El Niño stresses several breadbaskets in the same season. Import dependence peaks before the harvest.
  2. Summer into autumn. Import-dependent food prices rise, and food inflation stays high after energy prices ease.
  3. Into the financial system. Because people can’t buy less food, a supply shock turns into sticky inflation that rate rises can’t treat, and central banks face tightening into it. Insurers meet crop, cargo and business-interruption claims and pull back cover. Loan books in farming and food weaken. Investors reprice food, consumer and import-dependent sovereign exposure. Public budgets absorb emergency support just as inflation squeezes them. The continuity risk sits on no one’s balance sheet, so it is paid for in the crisis instead of priced in advance.

How the room is designed

  • Small and senior. Eight to ten people at director level with executive power. How senior is a design choice: the top, one level below or two, depending on who holds the remit to act.
  • A host that lends weight. Ideally the European Central Bank or the European Investment Bank, at their offices. Neither has been asked yet.
  • A conversation with everyone first. A one-to-one interview with every participant beforehand, to understand their exposure, what they could change and how they are likely to react. The design of the day builds on what we hear.
  • Rehearsed. The team runs the session in full beforehand, playing the participants.
  • In finance language. Plain terms, near-term horizons, no debates about terminology.
  • Fixed slots for senior voices. Steering-board members and other senior contributors speak in set places on the agenda, each with a specific ask.
  • Repeatable. Later rooms reuse the format with a different shock or a different part of the financial system.

Who should be in the room

Our first shortlist leans on sustainability and climate leads. They are our natural allies and often the way in, but they rarely hold the balance sheet. For each institution we should aim for the person whose decisions would change under this scenario: a chief risk officer, a chief underwriting officer, a head of agricultural lending, a chief investment officer or a financial-stability director. The sustainability lead can open the door, and the seat should go to whoever holds the remit.

We aim for people who direct capital flows: those who set allocation, lending, underwriting or supervisory rules. That usually means the C-suite or director-general level, or one level below where that person holds the remit.

Keeping it moving

The steering board sets the direction and meets at set points: before the Berlin meeting, on the final design before the first room, and at the debrief. Between those points, the coordinators and the operational lead put that direction into practice, and the operational lead runs the day-to-day within the agreed scope and budget. Delivery never waits on one person’s diary, which makes building a highly capable delivery team the priority.

Resourcing stays simple. Each partner receives a baseline, plus resource for the time its people actually spend.

Making the most of the interest

Talks on this theme are drawing more requests for conversations than we can take one by one. We want that interest to build the programme rather than use up the team’s time.

  • Every conversation is a pre-interview. The same short guide each time: what are you exposed to, what could you change yourself, who else would need to be there, would your institution host or fund. Notes go in one shared place, so each conversation sharpens the scenario and the shortlist.
  • A few set formats. The talk, for broad interest. A conversation, for anyone who might belong in a room. The situation room, by invitation. A room for one institution, which that institution pays for.
  • Ask for the person who holds the balance sheet. Many requests will come from sustainability teams. We welcome them and ask each to bring or introduce the colleague with the remit to act.
  • Count the interest. A log of who asked, plus any offer to host or fund, is evidence for the Berlin funding ask.
  • Keep the rooms small. A waiting list with dates for the first and second rooms, not webinars.
  • Run it from the operational lead, not the coordinators. Partners in the expertise pool take some of the conversations.

Funding

We will raise money for the programme from public banks, private banks and philanthropies. Some institutions may prefer to contribute by hosting a room. The longer-term aim is for financial institutions to pay for rooms that serve them. The first ask goes to the Collaborative’s Berlin meeting in early December.

Questions for choosing

  • Are we preparing for a specific shock, testing a policy decision or building a recurring capability?
  • Which decisions should participants make during the session, and how much modelling do they need to make them?
  • What can an existing partner adapt, and what should be reusable afterwards?
  • Who owns preparation, facilitation and follow-through?
