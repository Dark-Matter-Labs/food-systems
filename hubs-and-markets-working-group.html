Why the room exists

One table, three constituencies

The working group exists to connect London's food hub managers, the boroughs still trying to build one, and the GLA's London Markets team — and to turn what any one of them has learned into something the whole city can use.

The stated objective is plain: bring together hub managers, boroughs exploring the opportunity, and the GLA's markets planning team to share what's already been learned, name the challenges every hub hits in common, and find the openings to build hubs that can genuinely receive food at bulk and sort it out again to many downstream partners.

The prize on the table is larger than logistics. Surplus redistribution is the easy entry point, but the group keeps returning to a longer list — the inequity built into how food moves through the city, malnutrition, and climate risk to supply — with food positioned not as the end point, but as the catalyst that lets local food projects work on the causes of poverty, not just its symptoms.

Four hubs, one anatomy

Same four layers. Four different ways to survive.

Aggregation, storage, logistics and purchasing show up in every hub the group has compared. What changes borough to borough is who pays for which layer, and what breaks first when the money gets tight. Pick a hub below.

Lambeth Healthy Living Platform

260T food / year 46 groups + 3 social supermarkets Long lease, free tenure

Sited in a converted car park, HLP reaches downstream groups Felix and City Harvest can't reach on their own — 50% of Felix's Lambeth-borough food now moves through this one hub. A food-ambassador programme, an employability scheme and recipe boxes sit alongside it.

The social supermarket model returns roughly £14 of food value for every £4 spent — a genuinely strong ratio undermined by a structural problem: HLP lost council funding just as demand kept growing.

Where it breaks: the traded-income model closed for lack of funding. HLP has a lottery bid in for £480k over 3 years and leans heavily on corporate giving to bridge the gap — funding for a discrete project is easier to find than funding for business-as-usual.

Newham Council-run hub

CRF-funded Council-backed food alliance Solar + EV fleet

Strong political will and acute local need got this hub funded through the UK Shared Prosperity Fund's Community Renewal strand, backed by a council food alliance. Council carbon-offset money has already paid for EVs and roof solar, with the batteries pencilled in for genuine 24/7 resilience.

The hub's flexibility is its real asset: it has doubled as a COVID vaccination site and a distribution point for Vitamin D and infant formula — infrastructure that earns its keep between food crises, not just during them.

Where it breaks: the benefits and social value are easy to state in principle, but hard to convert into a frontline social-return-on-investment number funders can actually underwrite.

Tower Hamlets

No chilled/frozen storage yet "Meanwhile" site, 7 years Section 106 explored

The current hub buys mainly from New Spitalfields and Western International, with a stated ambition to buy healthier food more collectively and more ethically — but there's nowhere yet to hold chilled or frozen stock, or contingency volume for what's already been procured.

A local developer has offered a rent-free meanwhile space for seven years, with an existing relationship that should make relocating to the developer's next meanwhile site straightforward when this one's lease runs out. Section 106 agreements are being explored for longer-term tenure. The ambition on the table: four food-and-advice neighbourhoods across the borough.

Where it breaks: storage capacity, not appetite or partnerships, is the binding constraint right now.

Camden + Islington, Regis Road

~90 food projects supported Commercial wholesaler co-located 3-year meanwhile site

Islington's own attempts at a hub were repeatedly stalled by the lack of land — and a small pilot proved scale, not caution, is what delivers the economics. Camden's food team found the answer at Regis Road in Kentish Town: a meanwhile site big enough to host both boroughs' food alliances, which share enough ethos to pool resources rather than duplicate them.

The site is designed to sit a commercial food wholesaler employing around 100 people alongside surplus-food redistribution and a community enterprise that employs long-term street homeless people and other vulnerable workers — with the commercial partner absorbing much of the cost. Every partner food project on site has to provide wraparound support, not just a handout.

Where it breaks: the joint business case unlocked Islington's Community Renewal Fund bid — but moving from surplus redistribution toward genuine food-systems change, on a 3-year site, is a longer and less-funded ambition.

Felix Project · the logistics reality

Two kinds of surplus, and a funding gap in between

Felix Project's own depot network is the clearest illustration of why hub-and-spoke geography matters — and of the gap between what the current system can absorb and what's actually going to waste.

The UK's food system holds roughly 72 hours of stock at any one time — everything from manufacturing through wholesale to retail runs just-in-time. That's a deliberate design choice for cost, and it means the surplus Felix Project handles is really two different problems wearing the same name: food made surplus by a manufacturing or retail hiccup, and food left on farms by a genuine system failure — a contract cancelled, a spec too strict, a crop that outgrew its harvest window.

"The purple sprouting was five foot tall and bolted in the heat — you were only touching the sides. Everything else got ploughed back in that night." On a gleaning day, purple sprouting broccoli, Kent

Right now, Felix Project and FareShare between them can hold roughly 1% of the 3 million tonnes of edible food going to waste on UK farms each year. The constraint isn't appetite — it's working capital and warehouse footprint. Getting substantially more of that food off the fields is, by Felix's own estimate, an £8–10m infrastructure question, not a strategy question.

Almost no organisation is running this logistics model at scale — Street Box (with Juniper, in Newham) is doing a smaller version, and City Harvest is the only comparable operator. Which is exactly why the group keeps returning to hub-and-spoke: the closer the final depot sits to the frontline, the shorter — and cheaper — that last mile becomes.

The tension underneath it: Felix Project's mission is surplus redistribution, not farm-level systems change — but food and social justice are already the leverage getting hubs built. Closing that gap, deliberately, is part of what this working group exists to hold.

What a hub actually does, drawn out

Three ways of looking at the same machine — the flows of money and food through a hub, the same anatomy exploded out across boroughs, and the closed loop a hub makes possible once nothing has to go to waste.

The money follows the food, not the other way round

Producers get paid. Surplus donors pay nothing — the hub absorbs that logistics cost so surplus stays genuinely free at the point of donation. Downstream, procurement and retail pay cost-plus; food projects pay cost, or less, because a funded relationship is what keeps them alive, not a market one.

Billingsgate, Smithfield & the Royal Docks

The wholesale layer is moving too

While hub managers compare notes at community scale, London's two historic wholesale markets are being rebuilt at city scale a few miles downriver — and the GLA is deliberately opening the door for the two conversations to meet.

The move

The City of London Corporation is relinquishing responsibility for Billingsgate and Smithfield. The GLA is developing the freehold at Royal Docks, with new combined markets planned for Albert Island in 2030–31, bringing an estimated 7,000–12,000 jobs to one of London's most deprived areas.

Beyond B2B

Working with Claire Pritchard at GCDA, the plan explores co-located services — a Food Academy teaching entry-level hospitality skills, an employment accelerator with food traders and restaurants, and a shift from a pure business-to-business model toward genuine business-to-consumer access, including a community café.

Hub and spoke

The markets team is also exploring logistics and infrastructure on a hub-and-spoke model with satellite sites — the same shape the community food hubs are converging on independently, at a different scale. The team's related circular-construction work is visible at tippingpointeast.com ↗.

The pattern under every hub

Everyone can fund the capex. No one can fund the boring part.

Strip away the borough-specific detail and the same four constraints recur in every hub story the group has compared.

Every borough in the room has successfully raised capital funding for a hub building or fit-out. What consistently doesn't attract funding is the unglamorous layer underneath it — the working capital to buy food ahead of being paid for it, and the ongoing cost of simply keeping the doors open. Funders will back a project; almost none will back business-as-usual.

Newham can point to vaccination delivery and Vitamin D distribution running through the same hub that moves surplus food. Lambeth's social supermarket returns roughly £14 of food value for every £4 spent. Both are genuine social returns — and both are hard to convert into a frontline SROI figure a funding panel will actually sign off on. This gap keeps coming up as the single biggest thing holding funding bids back.

Most attendees have applied, or are preparing to apply, to the same climate funding pots — as separate boroughs, competing against each other for the same pool. The group's proposed fix is a single partnership bid, led by Sustain and tested across several hubs at once, so the boroughs stop bidding against one another and a working blueprint gets shared through the commons and replicated, rather than reinvented borough by borough.

Climate risk, health inequality, malnutrition, farmer precarity, soil health and the recurring need for emergency food all point the same direction: building hub infrastructure now delivers benefits immediately, and reduces exposure to the next climate shock, while helping UK-grown produce actually reach London plates rather than being routed around it.

FareShare's charitable objects only permit buying food once every other avenue is exhausted and it's genuinely become waste — even when a hub could step in earlier, underwrite the farmer's risk, and negotiate from a calmer position. One recent intervention paid 50p/kg for surplus squash; asked why that price wasn't offered sooner, the answer was simply that the rules don't allow it. Changing when a hub is permitted to buy, not just whether it can, is its own piece of unfinished work.

A 50-hectare Kent fruit farm was losing money on labour costs after Brexit — until Environmental Land Management payments for carbon-sink trees and pesticide-free rows turned a roughly £50,000 loss into close to £100,000 profit. The catch: every piece of fruit picked erodes that new income, so the most rational move is to stop picking fruit entirely. ELMS has since been paused for review. Any procurement protocol this portfolio proposes has to survive contact with incentives that specific, not just the average case.

Beyond the next funding round

Five years, not 2040

2040 is far enough away to feel safe to ignore. The group's next exercise picks a nearer horizon on purpose: what would a system look like that we'd be proud to hand to our grandchildren — and what has to be true in five years for that to still be on track?

The question on the table

How are people fed. What are they eating, and how does it reach them. How far have London's large supermarkets moved away from the practices that currently degrade the food system. And — put to Felix Project directly — could an organisation built on redistributing surplus eventually evolve into a piece of state-owned food infrastructure, rather than a permanent workaround for one that's failing.

Two working precedents

Brazil — under Lula's government, state-run hub-and-spoke logistics connect farms directly to public plates, with the state carrying the logistics risk rather than each supplier bearing it alone.

Sweden — a small municipality has piloted "preparedness procurement," where local farmers become de facto stewards of resilience: growing grains and legumes for municipal storage that doubles as a hub feeding public-sector kitchens.
