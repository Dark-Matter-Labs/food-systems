WHO THIS IS FOR

City-wide Mobilisation

Food systems resilience is built across multiple different scales. A coordinated effort has been mentioned multiple times as the most crucial element of a shared transition (be it in buying power of more resilient supply, distribution, local processing capacity or reserve). This call-to-all gathers actors to:

COUNCIL LEADERS

Use the most powerful local policy levers

Boroughs hold the most direct levers — public meals, procurement, land, local partnerships. This initiative aims to map the tools already within boroughs’ mandate and show how they connect to food resilience and preparedness.

LONDON — REGIONAL LEVEL

Pool buying power across London boroughs

Working across boroughs, in public health, regional development or infrastructure — this is about coordinating what boroughs are doing on the ground, and pooling the buying power no single borough has alone. The Greater London Authority and the NHS sit here too.

PRODUCERS, FOOD HUBS, CIVIC ORGANISATIONS

Scale and accelerate work already happening on the ground

Growers, grocers, community kitchens, food partnerships — this maps the policy environment that could support your work and the economic circuits you could become part of. The governance and financing case is being built around what you’re already doing, not instead of it.

NATIONAL GOVERNMENT

Build local proofs to encode new food economics

The enabling infrastructure — the frameworks and fiscal instruments that make local and regional action scalable — sits at this level. We aim to strengthen existing and build new coalitions to influence national policy.

WHY NOW

The food crisis is finally getting attention.

Food inflation is forecast to reach around 9% by the end of 2026, and the Food Foundation’s basic-basket tracker already shows prices roughly 30% higher than in April 2022 — a second price shock inside four years, arriving on top of the first.

The Joint Intelligence Committee and Defra have both gone further, warning of a strategic risk of catastrophic UK food system failure by 2030 — not a distant scenario, but inside the term of whoever is in office right now.

This isn’t the first attempt. The UK has passed roughly 689 food and health policies and 14 national strategies over the past 30 years, with no measurable dent in obesity or health inequality to show for it.

That’s exactly the moment London’s 32 boroughs risk running 32 more uncoordinated pilots — each rediscovering the same procurement problem alone, the way national policy already has, repeatedly. The pathways below exist so that doesn’t happen again.

There are multiple plausible ways to transform the food systems. The pathways below set direction, organise interventions, and actors around resilience, health, environmental, economic vitality, and equity & fairness outcomes — each one makes the next possible, and skipping one is usually why a pilot doesn’t survive contact with scale.

PATHWAY 01

Civic-led delivery, civic resilience and preparedness

Procurement protocols and buying coalitions don’t feed anyone on their own. Grocers, dinners, workshops and community-run hubs are the layer that turn food into fresh meals someone can buy or receive.

PATHWAY 02

Coordinated, collective buying power of organic, resilient supply

No single borough’s contract is big enough to move a farmer’s decision. A buying coalition of London boroughs and businesses is — turning committed public and commercial demand into the confidence a grower needs to convert land.

PATHWAY 03

New Food Economics

New economics of food through policy, economic, accounting, financing, and monetary instruments. Sounds complex — plenty of best practice to learn from.

PATHWAY 04

Nutrient density is the target, not calories

The baseline shifts from “enough food” to genuinely nourishing food — because a system that only solves for calories quietly manufactures the next public-health crisis while claiming to have solved the last one.

THE PORTFOLIO

What rides on each pathway

Every item below sits on one of the four waves above — hover a row to find its point on the wave. Demand makes healthy food easier to choose. Hub is the shops, pharmacies and services people actually walk into. New Food Economics is the instruments that make the other three payable. Supply makes sure enough good food is being grown in the first place.

Demand

P01
Constructing pull for a different kind of food — through money, protocol, and information.
Marketing Investment Fund
A cross-retailer fund investing in demand construction for healthy food and new food cultures, priced against the revenue uplift it creates.
Procurement Protocol
A shared standard that public and institutional buyers sign into, from the largest anchor institution to the smallest, without giving up their own local procurement.
Consumption Transparency
An annual receipt totalling spend, salt, and sugar against delivery-app orders — no verdict attached, just the number, the way cigarette packaging works.
Street Food Licensing
A formal micro-economy built around the most carbon- and infrastructure-efficient food format there is.
Default-healthy public catering
Collapse the choice down to healthy options only, rather than removing choice altogether — the simplification that makes a genuinely robust supply chain possible behind it, in school kitchens and beyond.

Hub

P02
The physical and civic nodes where supply and demand actually meet a person.
(Farmacy) Pharmacy as a neighbourhood performance-and-nutrition node
Pick up a prescription and walk out with a bag of seasonal veg, a free blood-pressure check, and a spot in Saturday’s community run. The corner pharmacy becomes the place you drop into even when you’re not ill — fresh food, real nutrition advice, and a reason to know your neighbours.
Public interest grocers
Next-generation, membership-model grocers with genuinely lower prices on a fixed cadence, not a voluntary top-up.
Multi-ledger ownership
Part-volunteer, part-paid, part-commercial contribution structures — a people’s supermarket that several kinds of stake can hold at once.
Collective buying engine
One B2B conversation standing behind dozens of community organisations — run like a business, governed like a non-profit.

New Food Economics

P03
The policy, accounting, financing and monetary instruments that make the other three payable.
The Camden food-value ledger
The glue the rest of this track reads from: a borough outcomes ledger recording who moved, which crisis never happened, which ward avoided what cost — credible enough that a finance director acts on it. Camden Public Health already holds an internal £8–12m/yr estimate of diet-related NHS cost by ward; evidencing the counterfactual is itself a fundable project.
True-cost accounting and outcome commissioning
One move with two faces: price what cheap food actually costs the borough, then commission community food against that price — paying for outcomes rather than parcels. The Social Value Act already makes outcome-weighting a legal duty, and the FFCC puts diet-related disease at roughly £268bn a year against £57bn to feed everyone well.
Prevention capital stack
Investors front the capital and are repaid only if verified savings materialise; residents co-invest alongside them at council-grade security. Two risk classes, one build programme — and both halves are proven instruments already, in Region Stockholm’s SEK 30m diabetes bond and Camden’s own £1m community municipal investment from 398 residents.
Universal Basic Nutrient Income
Turns the assistance tracks Camden already runs — roughly £2.4m a year of emergency food spend, plus the Money-first work — into good food as an entitlement rather than a referral. Universal free school meals already return about £1.71 for every £1 spent.

Supply

P04
The baseline production that has to survive the shock, not just look good before it.
Organic growth via collective buying
London boroughs and businesses pooling committed demand into contracts big enough to justify a farmer converting land — the buying power no single borough has alone. That same soil holds more water through drought and needs far less imported fertiliser — a resilience dividend, not just a health one.
Automated farm-to-farm coverage
A forecasting engine that predicts surplus and shortfall across a network of farms and routes supply between them before a contract breaks — one contract structure that scales unchanged from a single farm to a whole region, with regenerative practice verified and priced as natural capital on real acres.
Nutrient density as the baseline
A network of community aquaponics and hydroponics infrastructure, autonomous and self-managing, framed as nutritional security infrastructure rather than lifestyle.
Climate-controlled growing
Indoor and peri-urban production built for genuine winter resilience — not a romanticised return to 1970s self-sufficiency that two heatwaves would end.
Undervalued labour & assets
Recognising food’s essential workforce and community-value assets — from warehousing to leases that specify supplier standards as planning conditions.
Baseline food stack
Background food supply designed to hold when the “closed-loop” techno-dream can’t — the stack that deals with volatility, not just ambition.
WHY ADJUSTMENT ISN’T ENOUGH

What if the market-based food system we’ve relied on has already failed— and a fairer model, one that works for growers and nourishes communities, is what comes next?

The economics of food don’t move by tinkering at the edges — they move with a genuinely new arrangement of ownership, contribution, and price.

Look at the current food market as a product that’s failing and you get edge-adjustments: a nicer label, a voluntary pledge, food surplus recycling with CO2 certificates, that do a lot of good but do not change the underlying logic.

Look at the market-based nutrition as a product that has already failed — generating externalities and vulnerability faster than it delivers continuity — and the theory of intervention changes completely.

Public-interest grocers already show the gap: prices structurally lower on a fixed cadence, not “voluntary contributions” bolted onto a still-too-expensive shelf.

WHO HOLDS THE LINE

Governance that can’t be voted back onto the market

We need rules that stop this work being sold off or quietly undone later — similar to how some housing co-ops are set up so no single vote can ever put the building back on the speculative market (Mietshäuser Syndikat). The question is then:

Can food infrastructure be governed in a way that stays “with the people” and scales at the same time?

TIER 1 — BOROUGH

An independent Food Partnership

The Council keeps a seat at the table — funding, convening, advertising the work — without holding the steering wheel. A declared, public work programme replaces “come and collaborate.”

TIER 2 — LONDON

A coalition of boroughs

Peer boroughs — starting with a founding group of neighbours — take on shared standards, collective buying, and a common protocol, so the model compounds rather than repeats from zero each time.

The Future Food Dinner — an experiential, cross-sector convening format already tested in Camden — is the concrete mechanism for building that coalition one peer group at a time, not just its stated ambition.

DISTRIBUTED GOVERNANCE LAYER

Two keys, one lock

The neighbourhood association runs the place and answers to the people who use it. The London-wide association holds the other half and has one job: to refuse. Selling the asset or changing what it is for needs both halves to agree — so no single vote, however popular locally, can put it back on the speculative market. It is the Mietshäuser Syndikat mechanism, pointed at food infrastructure instead of housing.

TIER 3 — NATIONAL

Permission from the centre

A policy briefing routed through Oxford to national government, timed to a six-month window, arguing for the deregulation that lets already-brilliant local actors build without red tape.

A charter sits across all three tiers: what it takes to count as a genuine partner, and visibility between members so nobody drifts.

A charter sits above individual projects: minimum requirements to count as a genuine partner, and visibility between members so nobody drifts from the same goal. A food bank can be a member — but only if it’s providing real wraparound support, not a parcel and a goodbye.

The same logic extends to a borough market’s trader waiting list, hotel-development leases, and any contract long enough to lock in a supplier’s behaviour rather than just their price.

In practice, that means people nominate themselves for a workstream and declare, in front of the group, where they want to be in six months — soft accountability that turns “come and collaborate” into something with an actual deadline.

The “as infrastructure” move has done this work before — for urban canopy, through TreesAI.

This isn’t the first time the “as infrastructure” reframe has done real work inside Dark Matter Labs. Trees as Infrastructure (TreesAI) made the same move for urban canopy — building the funding infrastructure for something previously treated as decoration. Food follows the same logic, one step down the same road.

Dark Matter Labs’ role here is researcher, coordinator, and orchestrator of the work and the funding behind it. Camden is one of several London boroughs genuinely moving the needle on this — a partner, not a commissioner — each testing a local answer to what is, underneath, a global pattern. Cities everywhere are hitting the same wall: food fragility that no single council, or country, can price its way out of alone.

A CONCRETE FIRST MOVE

Set up a Local Food Resilience Committee — before the law requires it

In plain terms

Every council should set up a small group — public health, local food businesses, food banks, community groups — whose job is spotting food problems early and knowing what to do if supply is disrupted. Government is deciding by 5 October 2026 whether to make this a legal requirement.

The National Preparedness Commission’s 2025 review, led by Tim Lang, recommends every local authority establish one as a matter of urgency. The Cabinet Office is now reviewing the Civil Contingencies Act 2004 to decide whether to make it statutory — with a call for views closing 5 October 2026. A borough that moves first has already done most of that work before the law asks for it.

What it actually does

  • Maps local food supply chains — most places genuinely don’t know where their food comes from or where the vulnerabilities sit
  • Feeds local risk data into Community Risk Registers and the borough’s existing Resilience Forum
  • Acts as an early-warning system for civil society on food matters, tailoring national advice to the local context
  • Builds the cross-sector trust and expertise network a real emergency response actually needs

Who sits on it

  • Public health & environmental health specialists, trading standards
  • Food businesses across the system — present, not dominant
  • Civil society: food banks, Citizens Advice, faith and community groups
  • Co-chaired by someone with local standing — typically the Director of Public Health or an Emergency Planning lead

What coops are for

Cost-of-living, food access, and community ownership. Valuable — but not built for emergency planning, risk assessment, or public communication during a shock.

What the committee is for

Emergency prevention and response — working alongside the coops, the Food Partnership and the Borough Resilience Forum, not replacing any of them.

SEQUENCING THE RESPONSE

Three horizons, not one deadline

The portfolio is built to be true at three different speeds at once — because the people in the room for this work are living in different horizons already.

Now — before 5 Oct 2026

Shock absorption

Food-price volatility and nutrition inequality are already moving cognition and health outcomes. The immediate, concrete move is standing up a Local Food Resilience Committee and responding to the Cabinet’s call for views before it closes — not waiting for a council procurement cycle to catch up.

Next — 12–18 months

Coalition & charter

The Food Partnership publishes a declared work programme, four London boroughs sit down together on collective buying, and a policy briefing lands with national government inside its six-month window.

2028 and beyond

The labour shock

Automation reaches logistics and white-collar work at scale. Food-as-infrastructure is the argument for treating nutrition as social-contract security before that shock arrives — not the response to it afterwards.
