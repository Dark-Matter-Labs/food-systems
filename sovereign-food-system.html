A Sovereign Food System

A food system doesn’t become resilient through better intentions. It becomes resilient when the money running through it — who creates it, who gets it first, how it circulates, how it’s withdrawn — is redesigned to reward nourishment, preparedness and local value instead of cheap calories and leakage. This is a portfolio of economic and monetary instruments for doing exactly that, from a municipal kitchen contract to a central bank’s digital currency — grounded in specific Swedish institutions, law and live pilots, not a generic model. But the pressures it responds to aren’t unique to Sweden, and an incredible amount of what’s here — the instruments, the sequencing, the reasoning — carries directly into other contexts, the UK included.

The diagnosis

A mispriced economy, not a failing one

Food’s real costs — to public health, ecosystems, rural economies and future resilience — almost never show up at the point of purchase. That single fact drives five distinct, compounding failures.

In plain terms

The food that’s worst for us and the planet is often the cheapest, because nobody has to pay its real costs upfront. Fixing that price signal is the lever everything else in this page pulls on.

01

Preparedness & Resilience

Food production depends heavily on imported fertiliser, feed and fuel, while retail is concentrated in a handful of dominant chains — a system optimised for efficiency in calm conditions and fragile under shock.

02

Environmental Degradation

Emissions, biodiversity loss, soil depletion and nutrient pollution carry no price at the point of purchase, so the food doing the most damage often remains the cheapest on the shelf.

03

Healthcare Costs

Unhealthy diets are cheap and convenient by design, while preventive nutrition remains structurally underfunded — diet-related disease becomes one of the largest, most preventable cost lines in the welfare system.

04

Inequality

Healthy, sustainable food is structurally more expensive and harder to access, so the diets most likely to protect long-term health are the ones lowest-income households can least afford.

05

Rural Economic Stagnation

Value is extracted from food-producing regions rather than retained and recirculated locally — leaving rural places less economically viable and harder to sustain for the next generation.

The vision

Five goals for 2040

A sovereign food system means moving from “enough calories” to a society genuinely able to provide nutritious, culturally meaningful food within planetary boundaries — even during crisis.

I

Prepared & resilient

Less dependent on imported inputs, healthier soils, able to withstand shocks and global disruption.

II

Environmentally sustainable

Agroecological and regenerative production, shorter supply chains, genuinely circular systems.

III

Healthy & nourishing

Plant-forward, minimally processed diets that are the convenient and affordable default, not the exception.

IV

Equitable & fair

Fair compensation for producers, less power concentration in the value chain, food as a guaranteed social right.

V

Economically revitalising

Local value retained and recirculated, rural municipalities strong enough to attract the next generation.

2040Read the full scenario

By 2040, the turning point at national level is a Food Sovereignty Framework with a Universal Basic Nutrient Income (UBNI) at its centre — every resident holds a nutritional entitlement to healthy, sustainably produced food, administered through a NutrientBank that manages which foods qualify and adjusts that list as supply conditions change. It’s financed through a fiscal system rebalanced around true cost accounting: foods that generate public costs carry those costs, foods that generate public value are made cheaper. A national investment bank channels patient capital into regional processing and storage, and the e-krona — a public digital currency — carries UBNI payments directly to residents.

At regional level, healthcare becomes an engine of change: health impact bonds finance food-as-medicine programmes that prescribe fresh food through primary care, paid for out of the diet-related disease costs they prevent. At municipal level — where the transformation is most tangible — procurement criteria open kitchens to smaller producers, living preparedness storages replace static stockpiles, land is managed under long-term stewardship, and Food for Work programmes move people from unemployment into agroecological skills. Many municipalities issue local food credits, interoperable with the e-krona, keeping value circulating locally for months instead of leaking out through national chains within days.

The system isn’t finished and isn’t perfect — true cost accounting remains contested at its edges, and global volatility still tests the buffers. But the five outcomes the old system couldn’t deliver — preparedness, environmental regeneration, health, equity and a revitalised local economy — are no longer working against the grain of the economy. They’re what the economy is designed to produce.

Municipal level, the primary entry point

Fourteen local instruments

Municipalities hold the most practical, immediately actionable levers — public meals, procurement, land, waste streams, social programmes and preparedness responsibilities, combined with local knowledge. Expand any instrument below for what it does and a real example of it working.

Municipal Money & currency design Regional National
4.1Innovative Public Procurement

Use procurement thresholds, legally-justified award criteria (health, environment, resilience, animal welfare), and local-currency payment bonuses to steer public meal contracts toward local, organic and resilient suppliers — without breaching EU state-aid rules. In practice: Södertälje now runs rolling “preparedness as a service” contracts with regional producers, aiming for around 30% of the municipality’s food supply to come from them.

4.2A Living Preparedness Storage

Replace static emergency stockpiles with a continuously-used community pantry: producers place food into shared storage and retain ownership until it sells, while the system rotates stock automatically so reserves stay fresh and double as everyday supply. Precedent: Finland’s National Emergency Supply Agency runs this logic at national scale — this translates it to municipal scale, connected directly to local producers.

4.3Food as an Employee Benefit

Extend existing tax-favoured wellness-allowance frameworks to cover nutritious food directly — a “friskmatsbidrag” — giving employers a recognised way to subsidise healthy eating the same way they already subsidise gym membership. France’s meal-voucher system and Finland’s tax-favoured lunch benefit show the regulatory path already exists elsewhere.

4.4Food as Livelihood Support

Move social assistance from one-off food boxes toward food vouchers, nutrient credits, and eventually a full Universal Basic Nutrient Income — guaranteed monthly purchasing power for pre-approved healthy food, preserving dignity and choice. In practice: Montpellier’s Caisse Alimentaire Commune cut food insecurity among participants from 11% to 5%, paid through a local currency.

4.5Food for Work

Pay unemployed people, young people outside work or education, and people in rehabilitation to work in agroecological food production, land restoration and preparedness infrastructure — meaningful, labour-intensive work that a mechanised food system rarely offers. In practice: Barcelona’s green-trades apprenticeships move roughly three-quarters of participants into work or further study.

4.6Food as Medicine (municipal)

Treat the food a municipality already controls — school meals, elderly care, public kitchens — as a preventive health tool, not just a cost line. Sweden’s own school-lunch reform has been linked to higher lifetime earnings, with the largest gains among children from poorer households.

4.7Protect & Secure Farmland

Create protected food-production reserves — land held specifically for cultivation, equipped with water access, storage and shared logistics, leased to new growers and cooperatives on terms that reward agroecological practice.

4.8Land Conversion: Perennial Systems

Fund the shift from annual cropping to orchards, agroforestry and perennial vegetables on municipal or private land — production that builds soil, sequesters carbon, supports pollinators and pays back over decades rather than seasons.

4.9Local Carbon Credits & Transition Income

Let the municipality and local businesses become anchor buyers of carbon, biodiversity and ecosystem-service credits — rewarding farmers for public benefits alongside food, creating a new income stream that makes regenerative practice financially viable.

4.10Municipal Cows & Pigs as a Service

Procure grazing and landscape management as a service rather than simply buying meat: farmers are contracted to keep animals on municipal land for biodiversity upkeep, with the resulting meat feeding schools and care homes.

4.11Part-Time Growers & Civic Production

Open municipal food production to anyone — workers, retirees, students, newcomers, people in rehabilitation — for one day a week in exchange for pay, building food literacy and identifying the next generation of growers and food entrepreneurs.

4.12Large-Scale Composting & Soil Inputs

Turn garden waste and food residue into local soil fertility at scale, rewarding residents for the biomass they contribute and reducing dependence on synthetic fertiliser imports.

4.13Local Fund for Nature-Based Solutions

Pool municipal, business and resident capital into a fund that finances wetlands, agroforestry and soil restoration, with contributors receiving modest returns or local credits in exchange.

4.14De-Risked Transitional Ownership

For a service already outsourced to a for-profit contractor — school catering is the clearest case — set up a company majority-owned by the council from day one, with private capital covering the up-front cost of in-sourcing and repaid over time out of the contract itself. Ownership converts fully to the council once the private stake is repaid, without the council ever needing the full transition cost up front. In practice: Camden looked at in-sourcing school catering outright and stepped back because the up-front cost was too high — this is the structure that makes the same move affordable in stages.

Redesigning money itself

What a local currency is actually for

A municipal complementary currency isn’t a gimmick — it’s one of the fastest, cheapest ways to change behaviour when budgets and legal room are both tight. It shows up as an option throughout the local instruments above.

In plain terms

A council can create its own local money, spendable mainly with local producers and shops, and use it to top up wages, pay part of a supplier contract, or reward organic and low-carbon deliveries — all without breaking EU competition rules.

5.2What a Local Currency Actually Does

A municipal complementary currency can be designed to reward the exact behaviours a food transition needs: bonus payments for organic or local produce, “eco-demurrage” that loses value unless spent on sustainable categories, and guaranteed circulation within the local economy rather than leaking out through national chains within days.

5.2bDesigning the Currency

Four design choices determine what a local currency actually achieves: who is allowed to issue it, what spending behaviour it rewards, how it’s eventually taken back out of circulation, and who governs those decisions democratically. Get these four right and the currency does real work; get them wrong and it’s just a gift card.

5.3Local Currency in Public Procurement

A council can require part of a supplier payment — say 30% — in its own local currency without breaching EU competition law, provided the currency is open to anyone on equal, non-discriminatory terms. It ends up favouring local suppliers through genuine market attractiveness, not legal restriction.

Regional level

Where healthcare becomes the engine

Regions carry the healthcare cost of diet-related disease directly — which makes them the natural home for financing prevention as infrastructure, not just treatment.

6.1Food as Medicine (regional)

Diet-related disease costs Swedish regions tens of billions of kronor a year in healthcare spending, while only a few percent of health budgets go toward prevention. Targeted food prescriptions, medically tailored meals and dietary support for high-risk patients can be financed upfront through health impact bonds — investors fund the intervention, and repayment is tied to the healthcare costs it verifiably avoids. In practice: Region Stockholm’s diabetes-prevention bond and Region Uppsala’s cardiovascular-risk bond already run on exactly this structure.

National level

Nine national instruments

The role of the state is to provide the mandate, financing instruments, legal clarity and coordination that let local and regional actors act at sufficient scale — not to run the food system from the centre.

7.1A National Food Sovereignty Framework

A permanent coordination structure across the ministries responsible for food, health, civil defence, environment and finance — turning preparedness, nutrition and rural development from separate silos into one policy architecture with shared targets.

7.2A National NutrientBank & Food Eligibility Standard

A single public standard defining which foods qualify for every public food programme — UBNI, subsidies, procurement, food-as-medicine — based on nutrition, sustainability and resilience rather than brand or geographic origin, and updated as supply conditions and seasons change.

7.3A National UBNI

A guaranteed monthly food allowance, spendable only on foods that meet the national eligibility standard, phased in starting with households in food poverty before widening toward the general population — purchasing power with a floor under it, not a blank cheque.

7.4A National Fresh Food Subsidy (Friskmatsbidrag)

A simpler, faster-to-implement precursor to a full UBNI: a targeted subsidy for nutritious food, delivered through existing employer wellness-benefit and welfare channels rather than requiring new infrastructure from scratch.

7.5True Cost Pricing & Fiscal Reform

Use Sweden’s existing reduced food VAT as the entry point to shift relative prices: lower tax or direct support for foods that meet the eligibility standard, higher charges on ultra-processed products whose real costs currently land on the healthcare system instead of the shelf price.

7.6A National Food-System Investment Bank

Patient public capital for the infrastructure ordinary bank lending won’t touch — regional mills, cold storage, food hubs, agroforestry — evaluated against preparedness and resilience value, not just short-term financial return.

7.7A National Support System for Preparedness Storage

Fund a distributed network of many small local reserves rather than a handful of central state warehouses, with a paid “preparedness service” for producers who agree to keep strategic stock in continuous rotation.

7.8National Land, Soil & Water Sovereignty Programme

Treat farmland, soil health and water retention as strategic national infrastructure — protecting agricultural land from conversion, funding soil-building practices, and cutting dependence on imported fertiliser and feed.

7.9A National Programme for Food Work & Civic Production

A labour-market pathway that channels unemployed people into paid agroecological work, alongside a “four-plus-one” model letting already-employed people trade one day a week of their time for food-growing work.

Sovereign money, sovereign food

Why the monetary system itself is the constraint

None of the above is impossible under the current monetary architecture. It’s just harder, more expensive, and more dependent on private credit conditions than it needs to be.

In plain terms

Right now, new money mostly comes from private bank loans chasing safe, easily-resellable assets — not from investing in soil, food storage or rural jobs. Fixing that means changing how money itself gets created, not just spending more of it.

The barriers

AMoney created for private profit, not public value

Most new money enters the economy as commercial bank loans, chasing profitable and easily-collateralised lending rather than public value — exactly the opposite of the long-horizon, place-based investment a food transition needs.

BAsset inflation over productive investment

Credit flows toward assets that are easy to collateralise and resell — property, land, financial assets — not toward soil restoration or food-processing capacity, even when the latter creates far more real public value.

CThe monetary centre–periphery problem

New money flows toward cities and capital-rich sectors with strong collateral, leaving rural and food-producing regions — which hold the actual land, labour and production capacity food sovereignty needs — starved of financing.

DHow risk regulation underfinances the transition

Because everyday payments run on commercial bank deposits, the state can’t treat big banks like ordinary companies — which quietly biases the whole financial system toward protecting bank balance sheets over funding transition.

EDebt dependence and financial fragility

When new money only enters the economy through debt, the food transition’s financing becomes hostage to interest rates and credit appetite, not to what the transition actually needs.

FPayment-system fragility

Everyday payments depend almost entirely on private banks and payment providers, who have little commercial incentive to build in the redundancy a genuine supply crisis would require.

GBlunt side effects of interest-rate policy

Raising interest rates to fight food inflation doesn’t produce fertiliser alternatives, local storage or resilient supply chains — it just suppresses demand while leaving the actual bottlenecks untouched.

The tools

ASovereign money: public control over creation

Separate lending from money creation, so new purchasing power enters the economy through public issuance under a democratic mandate — not solely as a byproduct of private bank loans.

BCurrency sovereignty as policy space

Keep monetary authority — interest rates, digital currency infrastructure, crisis tools — within domestic institutions, so food-system policy has room to design tools suited to local conditions rather than inheriting someone else’s priorities.

CA public digital currency as infrastructure

A central-bank digital currency (Sweden’s e-krona) would give the state a direct channel to households and producers for payment resilience and targeted purchasing power — the infrastructure a national UBNI would actually run on.

DMonetary tools beyond the interest rate

With a public digital currency in place, institutions could influence money supply and demand directly, rather than relying entirely on hoping private banks lend more or less.

Implementation

Four expanding circles

None of this depends on one national reform or every actor moving at once. It starts with existing municipal mandates, and widens as capacity, evidence and political support build.

Phase 1

Most vulnerable

People in food poverty, welfare recipients

Food as livelihood support, food vouchers, nutrient credits, targeted UBNI pilots, community kitchens.

Phase 2

Risk groups

People with diet-related disease, elderly people, children

Food as Medicine, school-food transformation, elderly-care nutrition, regional health impact bonds.

Phase 3

Broader population

Everyone

Employee food benefits, fresh food subsidies, local currencies, procurement reform, food-literacy work.

Phase 4

Whole system

Producers, municipalities, the national economy

Land conversion, national eligibility standards, the investment bank, sovereign monetary infrastructure.
